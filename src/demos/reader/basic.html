A Short History of the Page

Before the scroll had a wheel attached to it, a page was a physical constraint: a sheet of a fixed size, filled once, then turned. The turn was free — a flick of the wrist, no scrollbar, no momentum to fight.

The web flattened that into one long, unbroken scroll. It reads well for a feed and poorly for anything you want to hold your place in. Ask someone where they stopped in an article and they'll describe a scroll position; ask a reader of a book and they'll give you a page number.

Multi-column layout can fold that scroll back into pages. Give a block of text a fixed height and let it overflow sideways instead of down, and the browser fragments the content into as many columns as it needs — each one a page, each one exactly the size of the box.

Scroll snap turns the sideways scroll into a turn: release anywhere near a column and the browser settles on it, the same small commitment as a real page. The dots below are the same kind of marker a carousel uses, generated one per column, lit up for whichever one you're on.

None of this needs a page count computed in script, a resize listener, or a virtual scroller. The layout engine already knows how much text fits in a box of a given size — pagination was something it could always do, it just needed a reason to.

Turn the page a few times. Every dot below is a real, focusable stop the browser placed there on its own — count them, and you have counted the pages.
